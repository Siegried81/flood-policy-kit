# Europe maps its flood risk well, and its flood preparedness badly — three fixes before the 2027 plans

**To:** DG Environment (Floods), the Working Group on Floods of the WFD/FD
Common Implementation Strategy, and the European Environment Agency.
**Date:** 30 October 2026.

## Key messages

- **25.7 million people in the EU-27 — 5.8 % of the population — live where
  a 1-in-100-year river flood reaches** if no dyke held, by the JRC's own
  hazard maps. **Behind the design standards of local defences, 7.3 million
  still do** — and they are not where the money is: the Netherlands' defences
  hold 99 % of its exposed, Poland's 26 %, Spain's 12 %.
  **63 % of the exposed live in a river basin shared with another country.**
- But the layer the Member States use to declare *where they will act* is not
  comparable between them, and two Member States are missing from it. A
  pan-European preparedness indicator built on it would rank reporting
  styles, not risk.
- **The flood risk management plans are reviewed on 22 December 2027.** That
  is the one open window. The three fixes below need no new legislation, only
  instruments that already exist.

## 1. The problem

The Floods Directive (2007/60/EC) asks each Member State to declare its Areas
of Potential Significant Flood Risk (Article 5), map them, and plan for them on
a six-year cycle. Europe therefore has two maps: where water goes, modelled by
the JRC on one method for the whole continent, and where States say they will
act, reported by each State in its own way.

The first map is good. The second cannot be compared across borders — and
most of Europe's flood exposure crosses one. In July 2021 the Meuse and the
Rhine basins flooded across Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands and Luxembourg in
the same week. A preparedness gap that only shows up when the maps are laid
side by side is exactly the gap nobody owns.

## 2. What we found

*We combined the JRC river-flood hazard maps with the GHSL population grid,
summed over the 1,345 European NUTS3 regions in an equal-area projection, and
laid the result over the EEA's declared flood-risk areas and the Water
Framework Directive's river basin districts.*

**Exposure is large and concentrated.** At the 1-in-100-year flood, 25.7
million EU-27 residents are exposed (16.7 million at 1-in-10, 30.7 million at
1-in-500). In ten regions, more than a third of the population is exposed —
Zuidoost-Zuid-Holland 71 %, Rovigo 61 %, Landshut 50 %.

**Defences hold most of it — not everywhere.** The hazard maps model no
dykes, so 25.7 million is an undefended figure. Masking every place whose
design standard holds the flood (JRC PESETA IV protection standards) leaves
**7.3 million at 1-in-100 and 26.9 million at 1-in-500**: most defences are
built for 50 to 100 years, so the rarer flood overtops them. Weighted by
probability this is about **207,000 people a year** — the order of the JRC's
own "160,000 exposed annually", reached from the opposite direction. Where the
gap sits is the finding: 99 % held in the Netherlands, Hungary, Austria and
Belgium; 26 % in Poland, 24 % in Romania, 12 % in Spain. And 62 hospitals and
2,849 schools stand in water the defences do not hold (355 and 9,491 without
defences; Germany reports no hospital locations to Eurostat).

**And we keep building there.** Over today's 1-in-100 footprint, built-up
surface grew from 2,917 km² in 2010 to 3,340 km² in 2025 (+14.5 %, GHSL) -
almost the pace of building everywhere else (+15.5 %), three years after the
Floods Directive was meant to steer it. The population inside rose faster than
the population at large (+2.4 % against +1.6 %).

**July 2021, measured against the same model.** In the EFAS reanalysis the
Ahr at Ahrweiler went past its 1-in-500-year discharge on 44 of its 55 river
cells, and every neighbouring German district past 1-in-200 or more. The
thresholds were fitted on 1992-2022, a window that contains 2021, and the
model under-reads small steep valleys (the Vesdre at Verviers reads only
1-in-20) - so this is a lower bound on how rare it was.

**The declarations are not comparable.** Belgium declares 99.9 % of its
territory as a risk area; Spain and Cyprus 0.004 %. Germany's 648 declared
areas total 76 km²; Belgium's 28 total 30,695 km². Germany is not four
hundred times safer than Belgium: Germany reports river *reaches* as thin
ribbons, Belgium reports *zones*. So Landshut, half its population exposed,
has 0.1 % of its area declared, while Dutch regions at similar exposure read
84-99 %. *(EEA layer, 2018 cycle, measured 8 October 2026.)*

**Two Member States are missing.** The current cycle of the Commission's
published layer holds 25 countries: **Ireland is in no cycle, and Lithuania
only in the 2010 one.** Both States do flood planning; the gap is in the
published reporting, and nothing in the service says it is there.

**Most exposure is downstream of someone else.** 783 of the 1,165 EU-27
regions lie partly in a basin that crosses a border, and they hold 16.3 million
of the exposed — 63 %. By the basin that covers most of each region, the Danube
carries 5.1 million, the Rhine 4.7 million, the Elbe 1.7 million, the Vistula
1.3 million, the Meuse 0.8 million.

**And the water is rising where people already are.** Across eight
bias-adjusted climate simulations, the median region's reference discharge
rises by about 20 % by 2041-2070, with the simulations agreeing on the
direction in 994 of 1,345 regions. 87 regions are both in today's top tenth
for exposure and rising — Groot-Rijnmond, Wien, Hamburg, Padova among them.
Article 14(4) of the Directive requires exactly this to be taken into account
in the 2027 review.

## 3. What this does not tell us

- **No State is accused of under-declaring.** A State may assess an area and
  find the risk not significant, which the Directive allows. The finding is
  about the *comparability* of a published layer.
- **Design standards, not guarantees.** The protection layer gives the return
  period a defence is built for, from 2019, for an older generation of the
  maps; a dyke can fail below it. The residual figure is "behind the design
  standards", never "after protection".
- **River floods only**, for basins above roughly 150 km². Surface runoff —
  much of what hit Wallonia in 2021 — is not in the hazard maps, so exposure
  is understated in steep, small catchments.
- **The cross-border share is a floor.** Switzerland, Serbia, Ukraine and
  Belarus hold parts of the Rhine, Danube, Vistula and Nemunas and do not
  report under EU law.
- These are screening statistics. The statutory maps are the Member States'
  own, and where they disagree with ours, theirs is the one the law reads.

## 4. Recommendations

| Action | Who, by when | Instrument, cost |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Fix the geometry convention** for declared risk areas — a minimum mapping unit, and reaches reported as buffered areas — and publish each State's declared share of territory beside its submission. | DG ENV through the Working Group on Floods, with the EEA; tabled in 2026 for the **22 Dec 2027** plan review. | An amendment to the reporting guidance the Working Group already issues each cycle. Staff time only. |
| **2. Close the two holes, and publish absence as absence.** Ireland's areas into the layer; Lithuania's current cycle reconciled; a service that says "not reported" instead of returning nothing. | The EEA, with the Irish OPW and Lithuania's EPA as reporters; before the Commission's **Article 16 report (22 Dec 2030)**. | Article 15 reporting, the existing legal route. |
| **3. Test Article 8(5) on the Meuse.** One riparian State reports a flood-risk issue it cannot resolve alone and proposes a solution; **the Commission must answer within six months.** | A party of the International Meuse Commission (secretariat in Liège), in 2026, so the answer lands inside the 2027 planning window. | Article 8(5) as written. No new body, no new money. |

Recommendation 1 comes first because the other two depend on it: until the
declarations are comparable, neither the gap nor a cross-border solution can
be measured.
